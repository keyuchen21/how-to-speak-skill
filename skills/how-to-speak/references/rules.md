# Rules from *How to Speak*

Every rule below comes from Patrick Winston's MIT lecture *How to Speak* (MIT
OpenCourseWare, published 2019; recorded 2018). Timestamps are `mm:ss` into the
video, so a claim can be checked at the source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Unzc731iCUY

Sections follow the order of the lecture.

---

## 1. Why speaking is worth study (00:19 – 03:15)

- The Uniform Code of Military Justice orders a court-martial for any officer who sends
  a soldier into battle without a weapon. Students deserve the same protection: nobody
  should go into life unable to communicate. **(00:19)**
- Success is determined largely by the ability to **speak**, the ability to **write**,
  and the **quality of ideas** — *in that order*. **(00:35)**
- Quality of communication = **knowledge x practice x talent**, and talent is the small
  term. What matters is what is known. **(00:55)**
- The ski-slope story: an Olympic gymnast, an outstanding athlete, was a worse skier
  than the lecturer, because he had the knowledge and the practice and she had only the
  talent. **Talent loses to knowledge.** **(01:20)**
- Learning to speak is non-linear. One technique out of a whole talk may be the one that
  gets the job. Collect techniques into a personal repertoire. **(03:00)**
- The method for building that repertoire: watch speakers who are effective, and ask
  *why* they are effective. Then develop a personal style from what is collected.

## 2. Rules of engagement (03:15 – 04:30)

- **No laptops, no phones.** Humans have **one language processor**. A processor busy
  with email or the web is not listening, and an open laptop distracts everyone nearby.
  **(03:34)**
- The speaker is also affected: a visible open laptop degrades the speaker's performance,
  which cheats the people who did pay attention.

## 3. How to start (04:30 – 05:40)

- **Do not start with a joke.** At the start people are still putting laptops away and
  adjusting to the speaker's vocal parameters. They are not ready, so jokes fall flat.
  **(04:40)**
- **Start with an empowerment promise**: tell people what they will know at the end of
  the hour that they did not know at the beginning. It is the reason for being in the
  room. **(05:04)**

## 4. Four techniques to keep in mind while speaking (05:40 – 10:20)

**Cycling (06:00).** Go round the subject, go round it again, go round a third time. Not
because the audience is unintelligent: at any given moment **about 20% of any audience
is fogged out**, whatever the lecture. Saying an idea three times is what makes the
probability high that everyone gets it.

**Build a fence (06:38).** Put a fence around the idea so it cannot be confused with
somebody else's. Teaching a Martian what an arch is means also showing what is *not* an
arch. Technically: *"my algorithm may seem similar to Jones's, except his is exponential
and mine is linear."*

**Verbal punctuation (07:30).** Because people fog out, provide landmarks where they can
get back on the bus: enumerate, announce the outline, name which part is starting
("the third idea is verbal punctuation"). Numbers give the talk visible seams.

**Ask a question (08:50).** A question is another way back onto the bus. Then wait.
**7 seconds** is the standard amount of time to wait for an answer — it feels like an
eternity to the speaker. **(09:09)** Choose the question carefully: too obvious and
people are embarrassed to answer, too hard and nobody has anything to say.

## 5. Time and place (10:20 – 13:20)

- **Time: 11 a.m.** Most people are awake, hardly anyone goes back to sleep, it is not
  right after a meal, nobody is fatigued. **(10:30)**
- **The room must be well lit.** Dim light signals sleep. Tell the audiovisual people to
  **keep the lights full up** **(11:30)**. When they answer that slides look better with
  the lights off, reply: *"it's extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids."*
  **(11:41)**
- **Case the place** — in the criminal sense, like casing a bank before robbing it. Ask
  the host to go to the room in advance so any weirdness can be handled or at least
  understood. **(11:57)**
- Before speaking, imagine every seat filled with **disinterested farm animals**. Then
  no real audience can be as bad as what was imagined. **(12:43)**
- The room should be **reasonably populated** — more than half full. Ten people in a
  large hall makes everyone wonder what is wrong. Pick a room of the right size; it does
  not need to be packed.

## 6. Tools: the board (13:20 – 16:55)

The board is the tool for **informing** (teaching, lecturing). Slides are the tool for
**exposing** ideas. Three properties make the board work:

1. **Graphic quality** — graphics are trivially easy to produce while speaking.
2. **Speed property** — the speed of writing on a board is approximately the speed at
   which people absorb ideas. Flipping slides is far faster than anyone can follow.
3. **A target for the hands** — novice speakers become aware of their hands "as if they
   were private parts that shouldn't be exposed in public", so the hands go into pockets
   or behind the back. Both are read as concealing a weapon, and in some cultures the
   insult is severe (a nun in a Serbian convent physically pulled the lecturer's hands
   from behind his back). With a board there is something to do with the hands.

Seymour Papert's trick: he pointed at the board constantly, and **none of what he
pointed at had anything to do with what he was saying**. It was still effective.

## 7. Props (16:55 – 23:57)

Playwrights are the custodians of knowledge about props.

- **Ibsen, *Hedda Gabler***: a pot-bellied stove sits on stage from the opening, its
  embers barely glowing. As tension mounts the fire grows hotter, and the audience
  *knows* the rival's manuscript is going into it. That prop is what is remembered
  decades later.
- **The bicycle wheel (Papert's)**: to show why looking at a problem the right way
  matters. Spin the wheel, apply torque to the axle (or blow on the rim), and ask which
  way it tips. Mechanical engineers reach for the right-hand rule, misalign their
  fingers, and get it right about **50% of the time** — an expensive education that
  reaches the level of a coin flip. Instead: tape one small piece of the rim, and think
  only about that piece. It comes over the top, gets pushed, so it must go that way; the
  next piece does the same; so the wheel tips that way. The problem becomes obvious.
- **The pendulum (Alan Lazarus, 8.01)**: a long wire from the ceiling holding a large
  steel ball. He held the ball against the wall, put his head flat against the wall to
  steady himself, released it, and waited many seconds while the pendulum swung away and
  came back to kiss his nose — many seconds in which the audience thinks *this man really
  believes in conservation of energy*. **Do not try this at home**: the natural human
  tendency the first time is to push, not simply let go.
- **The snapped pointer**: the lecturer once broke a wooden pointer on stage. Twenty
  years later, an audience member remembered that talk as "the one where you broke the
  pointer". Props are what survive. (The lecture returns to this story later, inside the
  slides section, at **30:22**, while contrasting physical pointers with laser pointers.)

Surveys of students always say: **more chalk, less PowerPoint (22:25)**.

**Why props and boards work — empathetic mirroring (23:01).** Watching a hand write on a
board actuates the viewer's mirror neurons: they feel themselves writing. Watching a
steel ball swing, they feel the ball. A slide or a picture cannot produce that. (The
lecture flags this as a personal "Lunatic Fringe view", separate from the speed argument.)

## 8. Tools: slides (23:57 – 36:00)

Slides are for **exposing** ideas, not teaching them — which is exactly what a job talk
or conference talk does.

**The Logan airport story (24:30).** A stranger asked for a critique of his job-talk
slides, unseen. The critique: *"you have too many and they have too many words."* How
could that be known without seeing them? *"Because it's always true. There are always
too many slides, always too many words."* **(25:23)**

### Crimes, and their fixes

| Crime | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reading the slides aloud | People know how to read; reading annoys them | Put fewer words up, then speaking is not reading |
| Too many words | One language processor: if they read, they do not hear | Cut to a few easily-read words |
| Speaker far from the screen | The audience plays tennis, head swinging between the two | Stand next to the screen |
| Background junk, logos, titles | Distraction; the title often repeats what is being said | Delete all of it |
| Bullets, clutter | No content, pure noise | Delete |
| Small fonts | Not mainly a legibility issue — a small font is *evidence* of too many words | **40–50 pt**; below **35 pt** is too small **(29:05)** |
| Laser pointer | Using it requires turning the head to the screen, so eye contact and engagement are lost | Draw a **numbered arrow** in the image; say "look at arrow 1" |
| Too heavy | Not enough air, white space or imagery | Print every slide and lay them on a table — the heaviness is instantly visible **(31:56)** |

**The experiment (28:00).** A student taught web-programming ideas with half the
information on slides and half spoken, then reversed the split for a control group.
Subjects remembered **what they read, not what was said**. One subject's after-action
report: *"I wish you hadn't talked so much, it was distracting."* **(28:34)**

**The laser pointer, in detail (29:38).** Point the laser at a projected image that
contains the speaker, and the audience sees the back of the speaker's head — no eye
contact, no engagement. A student watching such a talk said: *"we could all leave and he
wouldn't know."*

**Slides are condiments (26:26).** They support what is said; they are not the main
event, and the speaker is not a supporting act for the slides.

**The one allowed complex slide (33:57).** A deliberately impossible-to-read slide — the
lecture's example is a diagram of the complexity of governing Afghanistan, whose point
*is* that it cannot be understood — is permitted exactly **once per talk, once per paper,
once per book**. The lecture gives this a vocabulary word (*hapax legomenon*: something
occurring only once). A second one is a crime.

**Crimes do occur (34:30).** Real examples shown: hands in pockets; a badly chosen room
(the Bartos Theatre — down stairs, across an open space, along a corridor, into a dark
gloomy space: fine for a movie, impossible for a talk); and a conference talk in Stata
where the speaker stood far from the screen, used a laser pointer, reached **slide 80**
dense with words, and that slide was the **first of ten conclusion slides**. The
audience: the meeting's sponsor reading email, the co-sponsor examining the lunch menu,
and one apparently attentive person who on video was nodding off.

## 9. Informing: how to inspire (36:00 – 41:30)

A survey of who inspired people, from incoming freshmen to senior faculty:

- **Freshmen** were inspired by a high-school teacher who told them **they could do it**.
- **Senior faculty** were inspired by someone who helped them **see a problem in a new
  way**.
- **Everyone** was inspired by someone who **exhibited passion** about the work.
  **(37:42)**

So say out loud why the work is cool. The lecture's example of building passion into a
promise: a resource-allocation lecture opens with a map-coloring program running so
slowly that "the sun will have exploded and consumed the Earth before this program
finishes" — then, with the adjustment students will understand within 50 minutes, it
finishes in seconds. *Isn't that cool?*

**Teaching people how to think (40:40).** Faculty say their most important purpose is to
teach people how to think, then cannot answer *how*. The lecture's answer: humans are
**storytelling animals**. Story skills start with fairy tales in childhood and continue
through law, business, medicine and engineering. So teaching thinking means providing:
the stories people need to know, the questions to ask about those stories, mechanisms
for analyzing them, ways of putting stories together, and ways of evaluating how
reliable a story is.

## 10. Persuading: oral exams (41:30 – 44:00)

The usual reason people fail an oral exam is **failure to situate** and **failure to
practice**. **(42:12)**

- **Situate**: place the research in context — this problem is pursued worldwide, there
  has been no progress for thirty years, a solution matters to many other things.
  Situate in time, place and field.
- **Practice — but not with the wrong people.** Do not rehearse with officemates or the
  faculty supervisor. People who already know the work will **hallucinate material that
  is not in the presentation**. **(42:54)** Instead gather friends who do *not* know the
  work, and open the session with: *"if you can't make me cry, I won't value you as a
  friend any more."*
- **Flak is inversely proportional to age (43:41).** Older people understand where they
  stand; young people are proving to the old people how smart they are, and are "subtly
  vicious". A committee full of grey hair is the easy committee.

## 11. Persuading: job talks (44:00 – 48:00)

From two members of a Navy science board, asked what they look for in a faculty
candidate:

- Dolores Etter, instantly: *"they have to show us they've got some kind of **vision**."*
  **(44:57)**
- Bill Weldon, immediately after: *"they have to show us that they've **done
  something**."*
- How long does the candidate have to establish both? **Five minutes.** If vision and
  evidence are not delivered inside five minutes, the talk is already lost. **(45:40)**

**Vision** = a problem somebody cares about + something new in the approach. The
lecture's own example: the problem is understanding the nature of human intelligence;
the new approach is asking what makes humans different from chimpanzees and Neanderthals
— is it merely quantity, or something fundamentally different? The answer offered: humans
are **symbolic creatures**, so they can build symbolic descriptions of relations and
events, string them together, and make stories.

**Evidence of having done something** = enumerate the **steps** needed to reach the
solution. Not all steps need to be finished. Example shape: specify a behavior;
enumerate the constraints that make it possible to deal with that behavior; implement a
system, because engineers do not believe they understand something they cannot build;
then demonstrate it today.

**The sandwich.** The talk ends by enumerating **contributions**, mirroring the steps
from the opening. **(47:53)** That is the general-purpose framework for a technical talk.

## 12. Getting known (48:00 – 53:00)

**Why it is legitimate to care.** At a fundraising dinner the lecturer sat beside Julia
Child, and watched a stream of people ask for autographs or say she had changed their
lives. Asked *"is it fun to be famous?"* **(49:29)** she said *"you get used to it."* The
realisation that followed: *"you never get used to being ignored."* **(49:40)**

So: **ideas are like children — do not send them into the world in rags.** **(49:45)**
Packaging is a legitimate concern because it decides whether ideas are valued.

**The Winston Star (50:12)** — five things that make work stick, all starting with S. The
lecture's own PhD work on arch learning became mildly famous, and only years later did
it become clear that it accidentally had all five:

| Point | In the arch example |
|---|---|
| **Symbol** | the arch itself — a picture that stands for the work |
| **Slogan** | *"one shot learning"* **(51:14)** — a phrase that gives a handle |
| **Surprise** | you do not need a million examples; one is enough if used well |
| **Salient idea** | the **near miss** **(52:31)** — not *important*, but *sticking out*. Some theses have too many good ideas, so nobody can say what they were about |
| **Story** | how it was done, how it works, why it matters |

## 13. How to stop (53:00 – end)

Two separate decisions: the final **slide**, and the final **words**.

### The final slide

Bad endings, in the lecture's order:

- **A list of collaborators** — a wall of names invites the audience to divide the work
  by the number of names. Collaborators belong on the **first** slide.
- **A URL, or "The End"** — the worst: it can stay on screen for twenty minutes and
  squanders both the real estate and the chance to say who the speaker is.
- **"Questions?"** — often seen, never written down by anyone. Wasted opportunity.
- **"Conclusions"** — perfectly legitimate conclusions that nobody cares about.

The right final slide is labeled **Contributions**, because it is the slide that stays
up while people ask questions and file out. **(53:30 – 57:00)**

### The final words

- **A joke works now.** By the end the audience has adjusted to the speaker's voice.
  Doug Lenat's method: *"I always finish with a joke, and that way people think they've
  had fun the whole time."* **(57:29)**
- **"Thank you" is a weak move.** Nobody goes to hell for it, but thanking the audience
  — worse, "thank you for listening" — suggests they stayed out of politeness and had "a
  profound desire to be somewhere else". **(57:56, 58:13)** Once applause has started,
  mouthing a thank-you is fine.
- **A benediction.** Two convention keynotes are shown ending on *"God bless you and God
  bless America"* **(59:17)**. In the second, the speaker visibly presses his lips
  together to stop himself saying thank you, then gives a small salute.
- **A salute to the audience (1:02:04)** — say what was valuable about the time and the
  place: it has been fascinating to see what you are doing here, the questions have been
  stimulating, I look forward to coming back.
- **Conventions that mean "it is over"**: in the Latin Mass, *Ite, missa est* —
  approximately *"the mass is over, you can go home"*; at a concert, the signal to clap
  is the conductor crossing to shake the concertmaster's hand. What everybody does is not
  necessarily the right thing.

The lecture's own ending: *"I'm glad you're here, and the reason is that by being here
you have demonstrated an understanding that how you present and package your ideas is an
important thing. And I salute you for that."* **(1:02:45)**

## Numbers worth remembering

| Number | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **20%** | of the audience is fogged out at any moment — hence cycling and verbal punctuation |
| **7 seconds** | how long to wait after asking a question |
| **11 a.m.** | the best time to give a lecture |
| **half full** | minimum acceptable room occupancy |
| **3 times** | how often the key idea gets said |
| **40–50 pt** | slide font size; below 35 pt is too small |
| **5 minutes** | to deliver vision + evidence in a job talk |
| **1** | complex unreadable slide allowed per talk, paper or book |
| **first slide** | where collaborators are named — never the last slide |
