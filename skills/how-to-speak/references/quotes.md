# Quotations, with timestamps

Short quotations from Patrick Winston's *How to Speak* (MIT OpenCourseWare, 2019).
Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Unzc731iCUY

**Accuracy note:** these lines are transcribed from the video's captions, then punctuated
and stripped of filler ("uh", repeated words). Timestamps are approximate to a few
seconds. Check the timestamp before quoting one in published work.

---

## On why this matters

> "Your success in life will be determined largely by your ability to speak, your ability
> to write, and the quality of your ideas — in that order." — **00:35**

> "What really matters is what you know." *(on talent being the small term in the
> formula)* — **01:14**

## On the audience's attention

> "We humans only have one language processor, and if your language processor is engaged
> browsing the web or reading your email, you're distracted — and worse yet, you distract
> all the people around you." — **03:34**

> "At any given moment about 20% of you will be fogged out, no matter what the lecture
> is." — **06:17**

## On starting

> "What you want to do instead is start with an empowerment promise. You want to tell
> people what they're going to know at the end of the hour that they didn't know at the
> beginning of the hour." — **05:04**

> "In explaining my idea I want to build a fence around it, so that it's not confused
> with somebody else's idea." — **06:38**

> "I counted 7 seconds. It seems like an eternity to me to wait and not say anything for
> 7 seconds — but that's the standard amount of time you can wait for an answer."
> — **09:09**

## On the room

> "Keep the lights full up." — **11:30**

> "They might reply, 'people will see the slides better if we turn the lights off,' and
> then I reply: it's extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids." — **11:41**

> "I imagined that all the seats were filled with disinterested farm animals. That way I
> knew that no matter how bad it was, it wouldn't be as bad as that." — **12:43**

## On boards and props

> "Students always say: more chalk, less PowerPoint." — **22:30**

> "It's amazing how props tend to be the things that are remembered." — **30:22**
> *(on an audience member remembering, twenty years later, the talk where he broke a
> pointer)*

> "Do not try this at home." — **21:45** *(on releasing a steel pendulum at his own nose)*

## On slides

> "You have too many, and they have too many words." … "Because it's always true. There
> are always too many slides, always too many words." — **25:00 / 25:23**

> "I wish you hadn't talked so much, it was distracting." — **28:34**
> *(a subject in an experiment, after material was split between slides and speech —
> people remembered what they read, not what was said)*

> "You want the slides to be condiments to what you're saying, not the main event."
> — **26:26**

> "You know what, we could all leave and he wouldn't know." — **31:08**
> *(a student, watching a speaker addressing the screen through a laser pointer)*

> "One per work, one per presentation, one per paper, one per book." — **33:57**
> *(on the single deliberately unreadable slide a talk is allowed)*

## On inspiring

> "They were inspired when someone exhibited passion about what they were doing."
> — **37:42**

> "We are storytelling animals." — **40:47**

## On oral exams and job talks

> "If people know what you're doing, they will hallucinate that there's material in your
> presentation that isn't there." — **42:54**

> "If you can't make me cry, I won't value you as a friend any more." — **43:22**
> *(how to open a rehearsal with friends)*

> "The older somebody is, the more they understand where they are in the world — but the
> young people are trying to show the old people how smart they are. So: subtly vicious."
> — **43:41**

> "They have to show us they've got some kind of vision." … "They have to show us that
> they've done something." — **44:57**

> "If you haven't expressed your vision, if you haven't told people that you've done
> something, in five minutes — you've already lost." — **45:40**

## On being remembered

> "Is it fun to be famous?" … "You get used to it." — **49:29**

> "You never get used to being ignored." — **49:40** *(the lecturer's own conclusion,
> after Julia Child's answer)*

> "Your ideas are like your children, and you don't want them to go into the world in
> rags." — **49:45**

## On stopping

> "I always finish with a joke, and that way people think they've had fun the whole
> time." — **57:29** *(Doug Lenat)*

> "It's a weak move. You will not go to hell if you conclude your talk by saying thank
> you, but it's a weak move." — **57:56**

> "When you say thank you — even worse, thank you for listening — it suggests that
> everybody has stayed that long out of politeness, and that they had a profound desire
> to be somewhere else." — **58:13**

> "What everybody does is not necessarily the right thing." — **58:33**

> "You can salute the audience — say something about how much you value your time and
> your place." — **1:02:04**

> "I'm glad you're here, and the reason is: by being here, I think you have demonstrated
> an understanding that how you present and how you package your ideas is an important
> thing. And I salute you for that." — **1:02:45** *(the lecture's own ending)*
